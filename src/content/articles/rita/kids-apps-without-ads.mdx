The world of kids' apps is a minefield. Many that look "for
kids" are full of ads, in-app purchase prompts, and dark
patterns that exploit children's developing brains. Here's what
to look for and how to find actually safe apps.

## Why ads in kids' apps are particularly bad

It's not just about screen time. Ads aimed at children have
documented harmful effects:

### Children can't distinguish ads from content

Research is consistent: children under age 7-8 don't reliably
identify ads as ads. They process all on-screen content as
"true" information. An ad showing "Click here for 100 free
coins!" is interpreted as a real opportunity, not a
manipulation.

### "Rewarded ads" teach problematic behavior

The popular "watch this ad to unlock the next level" mechanic
teaches children that ads are valuable. They learn to seek out
and engage with advertising — exactly the opposite of media
literacy.

### Ads can be inappropriate

Even in "kid" apps, ad networks deliver inappropriate ads.
Studies have found violent games advertised in children's apps,
dating apps in puzzles for 5-year-olds, gambling promotions in
coloring apps.

### Data collection

Behind every ad is data collection. Kids' apps that show
behavioral ads have collected data on your child — usage
patterns, device IDs, sometimes location.

## How to evaluate a kids' app before downloading

### Step 1: Check the category

Look at the bottom of the App Store listing under "Category."

- **"Kids"** — strictest rules, fewer ads allowed
- **"Education"** — no special protection, common to have ads
- **"Games"** — full ad rules, often the worst
- **"Entertainment"** — varies wildly

Apps in the **Kids** category are strongly preferred.

### Step 2: Read "In-App Purchases"

If listed, scroll down. What's there?

- **Just "Remove Ads — $3"** = ads-free option exists, pay it
- **Dozens of options** ($0.99 to $99.99) = "freemium" trap
- **Subscriptions** = ongoing cost, but often ad-free
- **None** = best, fully paid up front

### Step 3: Check recent reviews

Sort reviews by "Most Recent." Look for words like:
- "ads" (red flag if mentioned)
- "in-app purchases"
- "scam"
- "trick" / "tricked"

Old reviews can be from before ads were added — recent ones
are more reliable.

### Step 4: Test before letting child use

Download, then use for 5-10 minutes yourself:
- Does an ad appear immediately?
- Are you prompted to "rate" or "share"?
- Can you trigger an in-app purchase by mistake?
- Does it ask for permissions it shouldn't need (camera,
  contacts, location)?

## How to set up your child's device for safety

### iOS

1. **Settings → Screen Time → Content & Privacy Restrictions**
2. **Enable** Content & Privacy Restrictions
3. **iTunes & App Store Purchases** → "Don't Allow" In-App
   Purchases
4. **Content Restrictions → Apps** → Set age limit (e.g., 9+)
5. **Content Restrictions → Web Content** → Limit Adult Websites
6. **App Limits** → Set total daily time on Games/
   Entertainment

### Android

1. **Settings → Digital Wellbeing & Parental Controls**
2. **Set up Family Link** (separate Google account for child)
3. **Block in-app purchases** in Play Store settings
4. **Set up Restricted Profile** if multi-user device

### Specifically blocking ads

- **iOS:** Privacy & Security → Tracking → "Allow Apps to
  Request to Track" → off. Won't block ads but limits targeted
  ads.
- **Both platforms:** Use an ad-blocking DNS like NextDNS
  (paid) — blocks ad domains at network level

## Why "free" kids' apps almost always have ads

Building, designing, and maintaining a kids' app costs money.
The developer has three ways to recoup costs:

1. **Charge upfront** — $2-5 paid apps
2. **Subscriptions** — recurring revenue
3. **Ads** — free to user, monetized via ad networks

When you see a "free" app, ads are almost always there. Even
"free with no ads" apps usually convert to subscription after a
trial.

The fair trade: pay $3-5 once for an app you trust, instead of
exposing your child to "free" ads.

## Red flags in kids' app descriptions

- ❌ "Watch ads to unlock more content!"
- ❌ "Join our community and chat with friends!"
- ❌ "Spin to win amazing prizes!"
- ❌ "Connect with Facebook/Google to play"
- ❌ "Limited time offer — buy now for $9.99!"
- ❌ "Free to download — premium $99.99/month"
- ❌ Reviews mentioning "ads" or "tricked" or "fake free"

## Why parental control alone isn't enough

Setting screen time limits and blocking purchases helps, but
doesn't stop:

- Ads within approved apps
- Predatory mechanics like "energy systems" that make kids
  return constantly
- Social pressure features

You still have to choose the apps themselves carefully. Limits
+ app selection together = safer environment.
