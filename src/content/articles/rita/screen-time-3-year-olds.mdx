No question generates more parent guilt than "how much screen
time is okay for my child?" Here's what 2026 research actually
says, how to think about digital time, and what separates a
good kids' app from a bad one.

## Official recommendations

### WHO (World Health Organization)

- **0-1 year:** No screen time (including TV in background)
- **2-4 years:** Maximum 1 hour per day, less is better
- **5+ years:** No specific time limits, but "balance with
  active movement, social interaction, and sleep"

### American Academy of Pediatrics

Generally aligned with WHO. Adds:
- Screen time should preferably be *shared* — an adult should be
  present
- Content should be *age-appropriate*
- Screen time should not replace sleep, physical activity, or
  outdoor time

### Meta-analysis of pediatric research (2024)

The latest meta-analysis shows screen time's effect on young
children depends **much more on what they watch and how** than
on the number of minutes:

- **Active** use (drawing, music, problem-solving) — neutral or
  positive effect
- **Passive** watching of fast-cut content — negative effect on
  attention
- **Solo viewing** vs. **co-viewing** — the latter always better

## Practical guidelines

### For toddlers 18 months - 2 years

Mainly video calls with grandparents. Otherwise, almost no
screen time. At this age, hands-on exploration of the physical
world is far more important than any app.

### For preschoolers 3-5 years

- **Max 1 hour/day**, broken into 2-3 sessions
- **Adult present** whenever possible
- **Active apps** preferred (drawing, music, simple puzzles)
- **No autoplay** — every video/episode should require active
  selection
- **Screens off 1 hour before bedtime** (blue light disrupts
  sleep)

### For early school age 6-8 years

- **Tolerable up to 2 hours/day**, but quality > quantity
- **Educational content** can be longer — homework, reading
  apps, art apps
- **Pure entertainment** (games, YouTube) shorter — 30-60 min
- **No screens in bedroom**

## What's a "good" kids' app?

### Green flags

✅ **No ads** (or only between sessions, no targeting kids)
✅ **No external links** that lead out of the app
✅ **No login required** — works without account
✅ **Premium features clearly marked** — child can't trigger
  purchases by mistake
✅ **Designed for fingers** — large touch targets, no precision
  required
✅ **Open-ended** rather than time-pressured
✅ **Encourages parent interaction** — sharable results, modes
  with parent

### Red flags

❌ **Ads** mid-app, especially video ads
❌ **"Watch ad to unlock"** mechanisms — teaches kids that ads
  give rewards
❌ **Forced login or social features** for kids
❌ **Loot boxes or random rewards** — gambling-like mechanics
❌ **Notifications** that pull child back to app
❌ **Data collection** beyond what's strictly necessary

## Common screen-time pitfalls

### "Just 5 more minutes" trap

Without clear end criteria, screen time always extends. Solutions:
- **Use a timer**, visible to the child
- **End with a "finishing ritual"** (turn off screen, sing
  cleanup song)
- **Have the next activity ready** so the transition is concrete

### Boring parents = screen on demand

If the screen is the only available alternative to "boring
nothing", it becomes the default. Solutions:
- **Have boredom-busters at hand** — coloring books, simple
  crafts, building blocks
- **Boredom is okay** sometimes — that's how creativity is born
- **Schedule active activities** (parks, walks) every day

### Screen as emotional regulation

Giving the iPad when a child is upset teaches that the screen
is the comfort source. Better:
- **Co-regulation first** — hug, talk
- **Calming activities** that aren't digital
- **Save screens** for joy or learning, not emotional rescue

## What about background TV?

Studies are consistent: TV running in the background, even when
no one is actively watching, is bad for small children. It:

- Reduces parent-child interaction (we talk less to kids when
  TV is on)
- Disrupts the child's play
- Trains the brain to expect background noise

If you want to listen to news or music, choose a podcast or
playlist instead.

## How to manage with your partner

Common conflict: one parent has stricter limits than the other.
Without alignment, the child plays them against each other.

- **Agree on a baseline** before the discussion comes up
- **Write down the rules** so they aren't subject to mood
- **Adjust together**, not unilaterally
- **Schedule "screen-free hours"** that both parents respect

## Quick check: is your child's screen time okay?

Five quick yes/no questions:

1. Does the child have time outdoors every day?
2. Does the child sleep enough (10-13 hours for 3-5-year-olds)?
3. Does the child still play with physical toys?
4. Does the child have screen-free meals?
5. Does the child use screens with an adult sometimes?

If you answered yes to 4-5, your child's screen time is likely
fine, regardless of exact minutes.

If yes to 0-2, time to recalibrate — not necessarily less
screen time, but more of the other things.
