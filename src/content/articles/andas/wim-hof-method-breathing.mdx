The Wim Hof method is probably the best-known breathing
technique right now. Dutch "Iceman" Wim Hof has made it a global
phenomenon, combining extreme cold techniques, mental focus, and
a specific breathing sequence. Here we cover the breathing part —
exactly as taught in the official method — and what you need to
know before trying it.

## Warning first — read this

Wim Hof breathing is a form of controlled hyperventilation. It
may sound dramatic, but it's accurate. You deliberately breathe
faster and deeper than normal, and that affects blood gas
exchange significantly.

**NEVER do Wim Hof breathing:**

- In or near water (bathtub, pool, ocean, lake)
- While driving or cycling
- Standing without support
- During pregnancy
- If you have heart disease, uncontrolled high blood pressure,
  lung disease, or epilepsy
- If you've recently eaten a heavy meal

**Sit or lie down in a safe place.** Dizziness, tingling, and in
rare cases fainting can occur. In water, fainting can be fatal —
there have been deaths from people combining the technique with
swimming or diving.

This isn't to scare you off trying. The technique is relatively
safe when done correctly. But safety rules come first.

## What is Wim Hof breathing?

The technique consists of three phases repeated for three rounds:

1. **30 deep breaths** — powerful inhale, passive exhale
2. **Breath hold on exhale** — as long as you can without
   discomfort
3. **Recovery hold for 15 seconds** — deep inhale and hold

Each full round takes about 3-5 minutes, and the total session
lands around 10-15 minutes.

## Step-by-step: how to do it

### Prepare

Sit or lie down. Preferably on an empty stomach (morning is
good). Turn off your phone. Have a timer ready or use the Andas
app which guides you through pacing and timing.

### Round 1

**The 30 breaths:**

- Inhale deeply through nose or mouth — fill both belly and
  chest
- Release the breath passively — like a sigh, without emptying
  completely
- Find an even rhythm — about one breath every 1.5-2 seconds

You'll notice tingling in hands and feet, maybe dizziness. That's
normal. It's CO2 levels dropping.

**The breath hold:**

- After the 30th breath, exhale all the air
- Hold the breath with empty lungs
- Relax — don't fight it
- Hold until you feel a clear urge to inhale

First-timers often hold 30-60 seconds. Trained practitioners
reach 1-3 minutes or more. Don't push — release when it starts
to feel uncomfortable.

**Recovery hold:**

- Inhale deeply and fill the lungs completely
- Hold for 15 seconds
- Release

Done with round 1.

### Rounds 2 and 3

Repeat the same sequence. Most people find the breath hold gets
longer in rounds 2 and 3 — that's the classic "Wim Hof effect"
where the body adapts quickly.

### Afterward

Lie or sit still for 2-5 minutes. Many describe the feeling as a
mix of calm, clarity, and energy. It's not unusual to feel
"high" — this passes within 10-30 minutes.

## What actually happens in the body?

The technique affects several systems simultaneously. The most
important:

| Mechanism | What happens |
|---|---|
| **CO2 decreases** | Rapid deep breathing washes out carbon dioxide. This raises blood pH (alkalosis) and lets you hold the breath longer. |
| **Sympathetic activation** | Adrenaline and noradrenaline are released — heart rate increases, you become alert. |
| **Oxygenation** | Hemoglobin is already ~98% saturated with oxygen, so extra breathing doesn't give more O2 — a common misconception. CO2 reduction is doing the work. |
| **Vagus nerve** | The breath hold stimulates the vagus nerve, activating parasympathetic and producing the calm effect afterward. |

The paradoxical combination — sympathetic activation followed by
parasympathetic balance — is probably why many experience the
method as both energizing and calming.

## What does the research say?

The research landscape is limited but interesting. The most-cited
study is Kox et al. (2014, PNAS), where participants trained in
the Wim Hof method showed a dampened inflammatory response when
injected with endotoxin. It's one of few randomized studies where
a voluntary method affected the autonomic nervous system and
immune response measurably.

Other studies have found:

- Improved stress tolerance in short-term trials
- Increased pain tolerance (partly linked to adrenaline release)
- Effects on mood and perceived energy

**But:** the studies are small, often with short follow-up
periods, and the Wim Hof organization is often involved.
Research suggests there's something there, but it's too early
to say "this cures X."

Medical organizations have not approved the method as treatment
for any disease. It should be viewed as a wellness tool, not
medicine.

## Common questions and mistakes

### "I felt tingling and got dizzy — did I do it wrong?"

No. These are expected effects of low CO2 and alkalosis. Sit or
lie down and let it pass. If it becomes uncomfortable, stop and
breathe normally.

### "I could barely hold my breath for 20 seconds"

Completely normal the first few times. The breath hold naturally
extends with a few days of practice. Don't push — it's not a
competition.

### "Should I combine with cold exposure?"

The full Wim Hof method includes cold exposure, but that's a
separate practice. NEVER do the breathing immediately before or
during a cold plunge — the combination can be dangerous. Wait at
least 10 minutes.

### "Can I do it before exercise?"

You can, but many find it interferes more than it helps before
hard training. Better in the morning, separately.

## How often?

Once a day is what most experienced practitioners do. Morning,
on an empty stomach, is classic. Some skip weekends.

Doing it 2-3 times a day rarely produces extra benefits and can
leave you tired or tense.
