Soil is your most important tool. More than sun, more than water,
more than variety choice. A tomato in great soil yields better
than the same variety in poor soil — no matter what else you do.

Here's everything you need to know without becoming a botanist.

## The three big questions

1. **What soil should I use?**
2. **Do I need to fertilize?**
3. **How does organic differ from conventional?**

## 1. Soil types explained

### Garden soil (outdoors, in-ground)

What you have naturally in your yard:

- **Clay soil** (heavy, common): nutrient-rich, slow drainage
- **Sandy soil** (light, common in arid areas): nutrient-poor,
  fast drainage
- **Silt** (rare): perfect middle ground between clay and sand
- **Loam** (the ideal): mix of all three with organic matter

For **vegetable beds in the ground**: mix 2-4 inches of compost
or quality garden mix into your native soil = dramatically
improves performance.

### Potting mix (for containers)

Factory-blended. Ingredients vary but typically:
- **Peat moss** (or coco coir — more sustainable)
- **Bark or compost**
- **Perlite or sand** for drainage
- **Lime** to adjust pH to 6.0-6.5
- **Starter fertilizer**

Easiest: pick "organic potting mix" from a reputable brand
(Espoma, FoxFarm Ocean Forest, Black Gold). $8-15 per 1.5
cu ft bag.

### Seed-starting mix

Lighter and lower-nutrient than potting mix — for starting
seeds indoors. Otherwise seeds grow too fast in nutrient-rich
mix and become "leggy" (thin and weak).

### Specialty soils

- **Acidic mix** (blueberries, rhododendrons): pH 4.5-5.5
- **Vegetable garden mix:** specifically blended (often a
  premium organic mix sold as "raised bed mix")
- **Cactus mix:** very sandy, low nutrients

## 2. Fertilizer — what and when

### The NPK scale

On every fertilizer label you'll see three numbers — NPK:

- **N** (Nitrogen): growth, green leaves
- **P** (Phosphorus): roots, blooms, fruit
- **K** (Potassium): quality, hardiness, flavor

Examples:
- **10-10-10** (balanced): all-around beginner
- **5-10-10** (low N, higher P+K): root vegetables, fruiting
- **3-15-30** (high K): tomato fertilizer
- **8-1-4** (high N): leafy greens, lawn

### Organic fertilizer (recommended)

**Manure** (horse, cow, chicken): the classic. Compost first
(let it age 3-6 months) — fresh manure burns roots. Apply 2-4
inches around plants in April/May.

**Pelletized manure** (chicken pellets): practical, less smell.
Sprinkle 2 oz per square yard every 4 weeks during the growing
season. Espoma Plant-tone or Garden-tone are reliable brands.

**Fish emulsion / seaweed extract:** liquid nutrient concentrate.
Mix into water, apply weekly. Mild — forgives dosing mistakes.

**Compost from your own bin** — if you have one, it's "gold":
free, nutrient-balanced, improves soil structure.

### Synthetic fertilizer (quick effect)

**Balanced 10-10-10:** mix 1 tablespoon per gallon of water,
apply every 2 weeks. Fast-acting, easy to dose.

**Tomato fertilizer (3-15-30):** potassium-rich, gives firmer
fruit and better flavor. Use from first bloom onward.

**Miracle-Gro 18-18-21:** the supermarket classic. Works fine
but doesn't build soil health long-term.

### Common fertilizing mistakes

❌ **Too much nitrogen** → lots of green leaves, few fruits
❌ **No lime** → long-term soil acidification
❌ **Fresh manure directly on roots** → burns the plants
❌ **Fertilizing without watering** → nutrient concentrate
damages roots

## 3. pH and lime

In most regions, garden soil is slightly acidic (pH 5.5-6.5).
Vegetables prefer 6.0-6.5. If your soil is more acidic, lime
raises the pH:

- **Dolomitic lime:** mild, mix 1-2 lb per 100 sq ft in fall
- **Garden lime:** standard, faster acting than dolomitic
- **Wood ash:** mild + free (if you burn wood)

Test pH if you're uncertain — a $15 pH meter or strip kit from
any nursery works.

## 4. Make your own compost

If you have a yard — start composting. It does three things:

1. Saves money on fertilizer
2. Reduces waste (food scraps + yard waste)
3. Produces better soil than you can buy

**Simple start:** An open bin 3×3×3 ft in a corner. Add food
scraps (no meat or dairy) + branches/leaves. Turn once a month.
Ready in 6-12 months.

The "browns and greens" rule: aim for 3 parts dry leaves/cardboard
(browns) to 1 part food scraps/grass (greens). Too much green
makes it smell.

## 5. Containers — extra challenge

Container soil leaches nutrients faster than the ground. Three
rules:

1. **Use slow-release fertilizer** (Osmocote, organic pellets)
   at planting
2. **Liquid feed** every 14 days in June-August
3. **Replace soil** after 1 year for annuals, 2 years for
   perennials

## Quick summary

For a typical home vegetable garden:

1. **Quality garden mix** + 2 inches of compost at planting
2. **Pelletized chicken manure** every 4 weeks during summer
3. **Tomato fertilizer** specifically when tomatoes bloom
4. **Lime** every other year in fall (if soil tests acidic)
5. **Compost** what you can — free money in your soil
