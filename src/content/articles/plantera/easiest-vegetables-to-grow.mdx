Not all vegetables are created equal. Some require early indoor
seed-starting, greenhouses, or daily watering. Others grow almost
on their own no matter what you do. Here are the 10 easiest for
beginners — ranked by "forgives your mistakes."

## 1. Arugula (Rocket)

**Days to harvest:** 25-30

**Varieties:** Regular salad arugula, Wild Italian arugula

Arugula is **foolproof**. Direct-sow as soon as the ground can be
worked. Cut the leaves and the plant regrows 2-3 times. Some
strains self-seed and come back the following year.

**Easiest mistake to survive:** wrong watering, forgot to
harvest, wrong location.

## 2. Lettuce (cut-and-come-again)

**Days to harvest:** 50-60

**Varieties:** Salad Bowl, Oakleaf, Lollo Rossa, Romaine

Cut-and-come-again means you cut the outer leaves while the
center of the plant keeps growing. Get 3-4 harvests per plant.
Sow new batches every 14 days for constant supply all summer.

**Tip:** Part shade is OK. Lettuce gets bitter in full sun and heat.

## 3. Radishes

**Days to harvest:** 25-35

**Varieties:** French Breakfast, Cherry Belle, Watermelon
(specialty)

Push seeds into soil, thin to 1.5 inches apart, water, wait 4
weeks. Done. Works on balconies, in raised beds, in boxes.

## 4. Peas

**Days to harvest:** 60-70

**Varieties:** Sugar snap, snow pea, English shelling pea

Direct-sow in early spring. Needs something to climb on (20-inch
sticks suffice). Pick when pods are plump (snap) or filled
(shelling). Sweet eaten raw straight from the garden.

## 5. Dill

**Days to harvest:** 45-55

Direct-sow, thin them out and you barely need to water. Big on
its own terms. Bonus: attracts bees and butterflies.

**Tip:** Let one plant go to flower — you'll get dill seeds for
fall plus self-seeding next year.

## 6. Bush Beans

**Days to harvest:** 50-60

**Varieties:** Provider, Blue Lake, Romano

Direct-sow after last frost. No staking needed (unlike pole
beans). Productive, beautiful, and they fix nitrogen in the soil
for next year's crops.

## 7. Root vegetables (beets, turnips, parsnips)

**Days to harvest:** 60-90

Direct-sow, thin to 3-4 inches apart. Can be forgotten for weeks
— they'll keep growing. Harvest when "big enough" (subjective —
pull one and taste).

## 8. Onions from sets

**Days to harvest:** 100-120

Onion sets = small pre-grown bulbs. Push 1 inch deep in spring.
Harvest in August-September when tops yellow and fall over. **Only
requirement:** don't plant them upside down.

## 9. Calendula (Pot Marigold)

**Days to harvest:** 60 (flowers)

Not food, but almost. Calendula is **medicinal** + **pest
deterrent** + **bee attractor**. Plant at the edge of your veggie
garden for benefits to all your crops.

Bonus: flowers are edible (great in salads, gives golden color
to butter).

## 10. Spinach

**Days to harvest:** 40-50

Spring spinach (March-May sowing) or fall spinach (July-August
sowing that overwinters for early spring harvest). Needs cool
temps — stops growing above 72°F (22°C).

## Honorable mention: Currants

Not in the main list because they take 2-3 years before first
harvest, but the **easiest** berry plant. Plant a bush, water in
year 1, forget about it. Yields 10-20 lbs of berries annually
for 30 years.

## What you should NOT start with

To be clear — avoid these your first year:

- **Tomatoes outdoors** (require lots of watering + staking,
  except cherry types in pots which are OK)
- **Hot peppers** (require indoor seed-starting January-February)
- **Pumpkins, melons** (huge space for few fruits)
- **Eggplant** (requires greenhouse or very warm microclimate)
- **Asparagus** (3-4 years before first harvest)

## How much yield to expect

A roughly 13×13 ft beginner garden with the 10 crops above can
realistically produce:

- 20-30 large salads (lettuce, arugula, spinach)
- 5-10 lbs of peas
- 30-40 radishes
- 10-15 lbs of beans
- 5-10 lbs of beets/turnips
- 5-10 lbs of onions

That's roughly $200-300 worth of produce from $30 of seeds.
